Travelling around Benelux by train: general tips

Greetings! The following are some information on train travel in Benelux (Belgium, Netherlands, and Luxembourg). Belgium built the first steam railway in Continental Europe in 1835. This was followed by the Netherlands in 1839, and Luxembourg in 1845. The three countries have well developed railway networks, connecting the vast majority of towns and cities. Within Benelux, rail is often the fastest way to travel between towns and cities.

Included in this page are some tips on train travel in Benelux in general, and also some on neighbouring France and Germany. Below are schematic maps I have drawn for the regular domestic passenger train services in Benelux in 2025 (Sun 15-12-2024 to Sat 13-12-2025). Some common themes

The following are some comments which apply to train travel throughout Benelux.

  • Railway companies have to give compensation over major delays that are not announced before the ticket is sold. This is EU's page on rail passenger rights.
  • Like many other European countries, trains that do not have compulsory seat reservations (i.e. basically all domestic train services) usually have two classes of travel: first class and second class. Most people travel in second class. You can tell which carriage or which section of a carriage is first or second class by a big number 1 or 2 next to the doors. The colour of the seats may be different (in Belgium this is often not very distinctive). The first class section of a train is also marked by an extra line on the exterior of a train. Notice that in the Netherlands, seats in the vestibules (the ends of a train carriage) are always second class, whereas in Belgium, the seats in the vestibules can be first class.

  • In some smaller trains, the first class section is just a few seats at the very front or very back of the train, with or without a glass door separating it from the rest of the carriage. Some smaller trains have no first class section.

  • Having a ticket, either first class or second class, does not guarentee a seat. With a second class ticket, you are not allowed to sit or stand still in a first class section while travelling.
  • QR-code tickets do not need to be activated. NFC (contactless) tickets need to be activated at card readers. Many paper tickets (e.g. Belgium) have a date, and do not need to be activated. Some tickets, e.g. Belgium's Standard Multi in paper form, requires you to fill in details with a pen. Some app tickets require you to press buttons to activate the ticket. In Benelux, nowadays it is rare to encounter paper tickets which need to be validated by sticking it into a time-stamping machine. These are somewhat more common in Germany and France. Paper tickets that need to be time-stamped for validation may say something like Bitte entwerten 'please cancel' or Hier entwerten 'cancel here' in German, and BILLET à composter avant l'accès au train 'TICKET to be validated before accessing the train' in French.
  • In Benelux, if you have a high-speed rail ticket (ICE, Eurostar, TGV), do not automatically assume that you can catch a slower train over the same trajectory. The high speed services and the ordinary rail services are run by different companies. (It doesn't matter if the national rail companies have shares in the high speed rail companies; they are still different companies.) If you miss your high-speed train, ask railway staff, they may be able to help you (e.g. your ticket may allow changes even after the departure time). See what NS International, NMBS/SNCB International, and CFL say over delay compensation. Also check out the websites of the individual high-speed rail companies.
  • Platforms are not numbered by the platforms themselves, but by track number. 'Track' is spoor / voie / Gleis in Dutch / French / German. At a station, if you see signs for, e.g., Gleis 3, 4, 6, 7, with 5 missing, that means that track 5 is a passing track without platform. The word for (railway) 'platform' itself is perron / quai / Bahnsteig. (Do not make the mistake of saying that, e.g., you leave something on the 'track' when you mean 'platform'.)
  • Longer platforms can be subdivided into sections A and B, or sometimes even C, or D. Different sections can have different trains on them, going to different destinations. Check carefully.
  • Sometimes trains can be decoupled, i.e. one conntected train splits into two trains. Check the platform section, and the destinations of each coupled train very carefully.
  • Most international train tickets without compulsory reservation specify which railway frontier point you have to cross a border at. (The border points are stated in the maps above.) 'Border' is grens / frontière / Grenze in Dutch / French / German (often abbreviated as gr / fr / Gr). Do not cross the border at the wrong frontier point. (Unlike domestic travelling, where there is some flexibility in routing if the normal route is disrupted, the border crossing points are usually inflexible for international travelling, as different border crossing points have different charges.)

The Netherlands: domestic and local cross-border

Companies

Most trains are run by Dutch Railways (Nederlandse Spoorwegen, "NS"). The second largest company is Arriva, which runs some regional lines in the north, east, and south of the country (plus some night trains). There are also other companies running individual lines, including some Dutch companies which run short distances into neighbouring countries, and vice versa. There is also Blauwnet, which is not a company but a brand; it belongs to the province of Overijssel in the east, and it is a unified brand for the non-NS services in and around Overijssel, run by several companies. See the maps above.

Service types

  • Sprinter (Spr): primarily all-stop services, run by NS
  • Stoptrein / RS : all-stop services run by other companies
  • Sneltrein / RE: limited-stop services run by other companies (a grade in between RS and IC)
  • InterCity (IC): usually only stops at larger stations. All ICs are run by NS, except: a) IC23 Zwolle – Enschede (weekday only), which is part of the Blauwnet system; b) IC Amsterdam – Berlin, which is run jointly by NS and DB (the domestic section of this has normal Dutch domestic ticketing).
  • EuroCity (EC): international IC, run jointly by NS and NMBS/SNCB (Belgian rail). The section in Netherlands (Rotterdam Centraal – Breda) has normal Dutch domestic ticketing.
  • InterCity direct (ICd): domestic IC that runs through the Schiphol – Rotterdam high speed line. There is an ICd supplement for travelling between Schiphol Airport and Rotterdam Centraal. (Rotterdam Central – Breda, and Schiphol Airport to/from other stations, do not require this surcharge.)
  • Eurocity Direct (EcD): ICd which extends to Belgium from Rotterdam Centraal. There is an ICd supplement for travelling between Schiphol Airport and Rotterdam Centraal.
  • InterCity Express (ICE): German high speed services. For domestic usage within the Netherlands, there is an ICE supplement on top of normal domestic fare.

The aforementioned types of train services are covered by the same domestic ticketing when travelling within the Netherlands, and the different companies running these services have very similar pricing structures. (This is different from Germany, where ICE/IC have very different pricing from those of the slower trains.)

Tickets

For the ordinary single and (day) return domestic tickets, NS and Arriva sell tickets to all train stations in the Netherlands. You don't have to buy separate tickets if your journey involves spearate companies. (Sometimes there are deals which are only valid on certain companies or lines.) The number of tickets do not run out, and there is no seat reservation. The only exception to all these is the Arriva night trains, which has its own ticketing, and they do not sell more tickets than the number of seats available (but there is not seat reservation).

When you purchase a ticket, you may be asked which company you want to use for your journey. Different comapanies usually run different lines. Even if they run on the same trajectory, the stopping patterns are different.

In the Netherlands, in the vast majority of cases you must have a ticket before you board a train; most usually you cannot buy a ticket inside a train. You may be fined if you are caught travelling without a ticket. As for the physical form of tickets, there are three main types: contactless, QR-code, and phone tracking.

Contactless

Contactless tickets require you to tap the ticket close the reader. The same contactless system is used for the vast majority of public transport in the Netherlands.

For ordinary full-fare second class journeys, you can use OVpay, which is tapping your ordinary NFC-enabled debit or credit card (of the Visa or Mastercard systems), or an associated mobile device to the card readers. Age-based discount can also be activated using OVpay. Arriva has a subscription for off-peak discount on buses and trains in Limburg province using OVpay. The range of usage for OVpay should widen in the future.

The tickets sold by ticket machines and over the counter are single-use OV-chipkaarts. They are of the size of a bank card, but made of thin cardboard. For the ordinary one-way and day-return tickets from a machine, and domestic tickets from a counter, a single-use OV-chipkaart cost €1 on top of the train fare. (A day-return fare is simply double that of a one-way fare, but the single-use OV-chipkaart still costs €1). At a ticket machine, you specify the origin and destination, date, and first versus second class. In some cases you may be ask which route you want to take if there is a price difference. For domestic tickets, it is not possible to specify a mid-way detour destination. For international tickets, it may be possible to specify a border crossing point that is different from the assumed one.

The NS ticket machines can handle tickets to all domestic destinations, most destinations in Belgium and Luxembourg, and some destinations in Germany (see the list of destinations). The Dutch ticket machines cannot handle seat reservations for IC and ICE to Germany. The Dutch ticket machines do not sell tickets on high speed trains towards the south (Eurostar, TGV), and can currently (late 2024 / early 2025) not yet handle tickets for Eurocity Direct to Belgium. The Dutch ticket machines can only handle full-fare tickets; for long-distance international travel, it is usually better to buy tickets online.

If you are already close to the border, using the foreign ticket machines found at some border stations are usually better than using Dutch ticket machines for journeys to those countries (if you don't want to or cannot get a ticket online).

Belgian ticket machines can be found at (from west to east):

  • Roosendaal
  • (No Belgian machines at Breda)
  • Maastricht and Maastricht Randwyck (but not at Eijsden)

German ticket machines can be found at (from south to north):

  • (No German ticket machines at Heerlen, Landgraaf, and Eygelshoven Markt. There are also no ticket machines inside the Dutch RE18 trains)
  • Venlo (full DB machine; tickets cannot be purchase inside the German RE13 trains)
  • Arnhem Centraal and Zevenaar (only machines for VRR / North Rhine–Westphalia; no ticket machines inside the German RE19 trains)
  • Enschede (full DB machine) (no German ticket machines at Enschede De Eschmarke and Glanerbrug, but there are WestfalenTarif / North Rhein–Westphalia ticket machines inside the German RB51 and RB64 trains)
  • (No German ticket machines at Hengelo and Oldenzaal, but there are Lower Saxony ticket machines inside the German RB61 trains)
  • (No German ticket machines from Groningen to the border)

There is also the durable OV-chipkaart. A durable OV-chipkaart usually costs €7.5. (This is purely a purchase cost; there is no deposit involved.) There are anonymous cards and personal cards. Anonymous cards can be purchased from many ticket machines and from OV-chipkaart service points. Anonymous cards can be used by any one person at a time, and you always pay full fare.

Personal cards have to be purchsed on-line. Subscriptions (e.g. discount subscriptions) can be loaded into a personal OV-chipkaart. You can buy the OV-chipkaart separately. or with some subscriptions you can buy one together with the subscription. Sometimes they provide you with an OV-chipkaart for free with a subscription.

Unless you have a post-pay subscription or a ticket pre-loaded into your OV-chipkaart, you have to travel by credit, i.e. load money beforehand into the OV-chipkaart. When you tag-on ("check in"), they pre-deduct an amount of credit. The amount can be as high as €20 (it depends on the bus / train / ferry company). If you do not already have that amount of credit in your OV-chipkaart, you cannot tag on. At the end of your journey, the difference between the journey fare and the pre-deducted amount is adjusted accordingly, i.e. you can get a refund, or you can be charged more.

With contactless ticketing, you need to tag on and tag off (inchecken and uitchecken). Transferring between different companies / systems requires you to tap off one company and tag on with another comapny. (A Dutch journey planner tells you if you need to do so.) Most stations only have card readers of one company. As shown in the maps above, sometime you may encounter two, or rarely three systems of contactless readers. Tap in and out of each company / system accordingly at the connecting station.

Tagging off and then tagging on again within 35 minutes is considered a transfer (a new flagfall is not charged). With a card-reading pole, you have to wait 2 minutes before you can tag off again to cancel a tag on. (If you cannot tag off, most usually that means that you have already tagged on.) Tagging off within 20 minutes of tagging on at the same station gives you full refund of the pre-deducted amount. With NS this time is 60 minutes.

There are two types of card readers: gates and poles. Not all stations have gates. At stations without gates, tap in and out of the system at the card-reading poles, which are standing objects with a circular area where you tap the contactless ticket. At stations with gates, make sure that you tap in and out of the system at the correct side of the gate. (For instance, if you arrive from a foreign country by train, do not use a contactless ticket to get out of a station if the ticket does not have a corresponding tapping in record from within the Netherlands. If you do so, there will be a penalty for irregular usage.) Sometimes there are card readers standing on a platform, with a sign saying overstappen above the readers. These are for overstappen "transfer", i.e. when transferring between different companies / systems, or when you arrive from a foreign country by train, having used up the international ticket, and wish to continue as a domestic journey in the Netherlands using a contactless payment method.

There are Dutch OV-chipkaart readers at the following stations in Germany close to the Dutch border. You can use normal Dutch contactless payment methods travelling between the Netherlands and these German stations (but not for travel within Germany). From south to north these are:

  • Herzogenrath, Aachen West, and Aachen Hbf on RE18
  • Emmerich-Elten, Emmerich, and Praest on RE19
  • Gronau on RB51 or RB64
  • Weener, and Leer (Ostfriesland) (curently there are only replacement buses running, but you can still use Dutch OV-chipkaart)

(Do not count on there being machines to load credit into your OV-chipkaart in Germany. Preload enough credit while in the Netherlands, or use some online or automatic credit-loading method.)

Currently OV-pay (bankcard / mobile pay) cannot handle first class travel. For durable OV-chipkaarts, they are set to second class travel by default. To switch to first class travel, do so at a ticket machine, or in the railway company app if your card is linked to an online account with them.

QR-code

Tickets sold on the internet has a QR-code. Tickets can be purchased from the NS website, or from apps like the NS app, Arriva's glimble app, the 9292 app, the Gaiyo app. For international travel, there are e.g. the NS International website and app.

The QR-code can be used to open station gates (the same reader is used for reading QR-codes and contactless tickets). If there are no gates, there is nothing you need to do; just board the train. Sometimes there are conductors checking tickets in the trains. (QR-code tickets have a date on them, and you do not have to activate them.)

German long-distance and Belgian tickets for the Netherlands usually include a QR-code for opening Dutch station gates. If you arrive on a German local train, in most cases you arrive in a station without gates. In Enschede you arrive on a platform that is not gated (the platforms for Dutch trains are gated). With RE19 at Arnhem Centraal, you arrive within the gated area. If needed, a QR-code can be obtained from the conductor as the train travels between Zevenaar and Arnhem Centraal. In the other direction, if needed you can ask the staff at the international ticket counter at Arnhem Centraal for a QR-code (explain your situation to them). There are also intercom buttons close to all ticket gates; press the blue button (not the emergency red button unless you have an emergency), explain the situation to the operator (voice only), and they will open the gates for you.

Location tracking

This works with Arriva's glimble app, and only in parts of the country. With a credit card tied to the app, and with a phone: a) that is not in flight mode; b) with mobile internet switched on; and c) with location tracking switched on, you can pay by simply swiping in in the app before you board the train / bus to begin the journey, and swiping off at the end of the journey. The app records your journey, and charges your credit card accordingly. Swiping is not needed if you are simply transferring between Arriva trains / buses. Currently this works for: a) Arriva trains in Friesland and Groningen provinces; b) Arriva buses and trains in Limburg province, including the RS11 train to Nijmegen; and c) RE18 in Netherlands and Germany (Eijsden to Aachen Hbf), and RE 1 or RE 9 between Aachen Hbf and Köln Hbf (Cologne). There are also e.g. day tickets, which also require you to swipe in and out in the app, without the need to tie a credit card to the app. A QR-code is generated for getting through railway station gates.

Pricing

Do I have to buy a ticket early? It depends.

NS has PriceTime Deal (Prijstijd Deal): for off-peak journeys, there can be a discount of up to 60% if you book at least one day ahead online (website / app). Each journey has to be booked separately; there is no return deal. Not all destinations are available, e.g. journeys that involve Schiphol Airport (even for just transfer) have no discount. An e-ticket is issued, in the form of an e-mail and/or loaded into the NS app. A time period during off-peak is given for the passenger to fulfill the trip. For shorter trips, there may be many departures which satisfy the time limit, but for longer trips, there may be only one or two departures possible.

If you travel with someone with a NS suscription, you may qualify for samenreiskorting, which is a discount of 40% off the full fare.

Different companies have slightly different pricing (sometimes different sections have different pricing). The normal single and return tickets have distance-based pricing by kilometer, or by tariff unit. A flagfall or a minimum number of tariff units is charged. When a journey involves transferring between two or more companies / systems, only the flagfall / minimum tariff unit charge of the first applies. (Hence the fare in one direction can be different from that in the opposite direction.

This is NS's map of tariff units. (Other companies have different charges for the normal single and return tickets on their sections.) One tariff unit is approximately 1 km, but the units are adjusted so that in many cases, travelling to the opposite end of a loop costs the same either way (check with the NS journey planner to see whether this is the case.) For instance, going from Breda to Utrecht Centraal costs the same going west via Rotterdam Centraal, or east via 's-Hertogenbosch. Similarly, going from Amsterdam Centraal to Rotterdam Centraal, the price is the same travelling west on the InterCity via Den Haag HS, or east on the Sprinter via Breukelen / Woerden. Travelling in the middle with Sprinter to Schiphol Airport, and then InterCity direct or Eurocity Direct to Rotterdam Centraal, also has the same base price before the ICd surcharge (see below).

For normal domestic travel, there are two kinds of supplement (i.e. surcharge).

  • InterCity direct supplement (ICd toeslag) €0 / €1.8 / €3. There is a surchage if you travel domestically on an InterCity direct or Eurocity Direct service on the high speed rail line between Schiphol Airport and Rotterdam Centraal. This supplement is not applicable to, e.g., children travelling on Rairunner or Kids Vrij, passengers travelling on an international ticket.
  • InterCity Express supplement (ICE toeslag) €0 / €3. Travelling on the ICE within the Netherlands is just the normal domestic fare plus €3 one-way. This supplement is not applicable to, e.g., children travelling on Railrunner, holders of many NS subscriptions.

There are the various types of domestic tickets sold by NS, e.g. tourist-oriented, day-unlimited, child, pet, bicycles. (Fully folded bicycles travel for free.) Not mentioned in that page is the off-peak youth day ticket (12–18 yo inclusive), which is an app-only ticket.

There is the Euregio ticket, which is valid on public transport in Dutch Limburg south of (and including) Roermond, and large areas in neighbouring Belgium and Germany.

There are the various types of subscriptions sold by NS (i.e. season tickets, rail cards). For instance, Dal Voordeel is less than €6 per month, and gives a discount of 40% off off-peak (the OV-chipkaart is tagged-on during off-peak, not necessarily the travelling). Off-peak is weekdays 9am – 4pm, 6:30pm – 6:30 am, and whole day weekend / holiday. The subscription fee and fares are charged afterward each month. Most of these subscriptions are valid throughout Netherlands, including on trains of other companies.

There are various types of tickets and supscriptions sold by Arriva. Many of these are region specific.

With many subscriptions, there is the samenreiskorting: three other travellers can travel with the subscription holder during off-peak periods, and they receive a 40% discount off the full fare. (For the subscription holder, the discount depends on the tagging-on time of the OV-chipkaart; for the co-travellers, the discount applies only when the whole journey is conducted during off-peak time according to scheduled departure and arrival times.) The discount for the co-travellers have to be organised online; each co-traveller is issued a QR-code, which comes in an e-mail and/or is loaded into their NS app.

Travel assistance

See NS's page on Travel Assistance (domestic) and Travel Assistance (international). Sprinter trains are usually accessible. On the other hand, many Intercity train doors have large steps. Travel assistance is available at most stations, available for all services at that station, including night services. Accessible toilets are available in the vast majority of trains nowadays. (Whether they work or not is another question.)

The Netherlands: long-distance international

The following is a list of long distance international rail services that run directly to/from the Netherlands. Many are bookable through NS international (website / app), or (the international versions of) the websites and apps of the railway companies of neighbouring countries. With all of the services listed below, it is a good idea to book early. With cheaper tickets, you typically have to catch a specific train (check the conditions carefully before purchasing a ticket).

The long-distance international passenger rail market is being liberalised in the Netherlands; the following list is not necessarily up to date (by the time you read this, there could be new services, or some could have gone bust).

  • IC Amsterdam Centraal – Berlin Ostbf. Within the Netherlands, normal Dutch domestic ticketing applies, but notice that international passengers may have reserved seats. DB-type dynamic pricing applies for international and Germany domestic journeys.
  • ICE International Amsterdam Centraal – Frankfurt am Main Hbf. One service per day is Amsterdam Centraal – Frankfurt Flughafen Fernbf – München Hbf instead. Seat reservation is not compulsory with 2nd class. Within the Netherlands, normal Dutch domestic ticketing applies, plus a €3 ICE supplement (waived for holders of many NS supscriptions). DB-type dynamic pricing applies for international and Germany domestic journeys.
  • Nightjet of ÖBB (Austrian Railway). Night trains between the Netherlands and Vienna / Munich / Innsbruck (you must book a bed), and Basel / Zürich (seat as InterCity, or bed as Nighjet).
  • European Sleeper, night trains to Berlin / Dresden / Prague, and seasonal trains to Venice.
  • GreenCity Trip, packages of 4 to 6 days, including two overnight train trips, and an added option of 1 to 3 nights hotel accomodation in various cities in Europe.
  • EuroCity and Eurocity Direct. EuroCity is the international InterCity service Rotterdam Centraal – Breda – [...] – Brussels South. Eurocity Direct is a service faster than EC, but slower than Eurostar: (Lelystad Centrum – Almere Buiten - Almere Centrum –) Amsterdam Zuid – Schiphol Airport – Rotterdam Centraal – Antwerpen-Centraal – Brussles South. Within the Netherlands, normal domestic fare applies with EC and EcD. With EcD, there is an ICd supplement of €0 / €1.8 / €3 between Schiphol Airport and Rotterdam Centraal for domestic tickets. Tickets for EcD can not yet be purchased at ticket machines. The NS Flex Traject Vrij subscription is not valid on EcD between Rotterdam Centraal and the Hazeldonk border.
  • Eurostar to e.g. Brussels, Paris, London. There are also limited services to Paris CDG airport and Disneyland, and seasonal services to the French Alps and the Mediterranean Coast. Tickets are not bookable for journeys within the Netherlands. For services to London, UK immigration is conducted before reaching Britain, and it is not possible to book tickets on such services to a destination in Belgium or France in between. (There is no such restriction in the opposite direction. Currently Eurostar does not stop at any other stations in UK.)
  • Also check, e.g., Festival Travel for train / bus / plane travels to music festivals around Europe, and Treinreiswinkel for all sorts of train trips, some of which are their charters.

Also check the railway companies of nearby countries for ideas. Germany, Belgium, and France have better international rail connections than the Netherlands. For Luxembourg, it is usually quicker to reach Luxembourg via Belgium (e.g. IC Brussels – Luxembourg, IC Liège – Luxembourg). However, if you are in the Netherlands close to the German border, it is also worth checking routes via Germany (e.g. RE Koblenz – Luxembourg).

Belgium: domestic and local cross-border

The first note is that, like Belgium in general, Belgian Railways has strict language policies. In the Dutch language area in the north, only the Dutch versions of the Belgian place names are shown and announced. In the French language area in the south, only the French versions of Belgian place names are shown and announced. Signage and announcements are in French and Dutch in Brussels Capital Region. There is also a small German area in the east of the country. The trouble for people unfamiliar with all these is that the place names can look and sound rather different in different languages. For instance, Mons is displayed and announced as Bergen if you are in the Dutch-speaking part of Belgium. Liège is Luik in Dutch and Lüttich in German. Antwerpen is Anvers in French and Antwerp in English. Leuven is Louvain in French, but Louvain-La-Neuve is a totally different place (and Louvain-La-Neuve is not translated into Dutch). The language boundaries, and alternative place names in Dutch / English / French / German / Luxembourgish are shown in the maps above, with the Belgian ones repeated below.

The largest station in Belgium is Brussel-Zuid / Bruxelles-Midi. In English this is sometimes referred as Brussels-South.


Companies

Most trains are run by Belgian Railways (Nationale Maatschappij der Belgische Spoorwegen / Société nationale des chemins de fer belges / Nationale Gesellschaft der Belgischen Eisenbahnen (NMBS in Dutch, SNCB in French). Close to the Luxembourgish border, services between Athus and Luxembourg are run by Luxembourgish Railways (CFL). At Arlon one also sometime sees Luxembourgish trains. Dutch Arriva trains run between Liège and the Netherlands, run in coorporation with Belgian Railways. The Eurocity to Netherlands is run by Belgian trains, and the Eurocity Direct is run by Dutch trains. Both of these are joint-ventures of Belgian Railways and Dutch Railways.

Service types

  • S: S trains are primarily all-stop services around the five big cities of Brussles, Antwerp, Liège, Charleroi, and Ghent. They carry an S number, e.g. S 1 is the (nearly) all-stop service from Nivelles in the south. through Burssels, to Antwerpen-Centraal in the north.
  • L: L trains are primarily all-stop services elsewhere in the country. In the maps, S and L lines are drawn alike; L lines are the ones without an S number.
  • InterCity (IC): primarily stops at larger stations.
  • EuroCity (EC): international IC to Breda and Rotterdam Centraal in the Netherlands. Within Belgium, normal Belgian domestic ticketing applies.
  • P: P trains are individual services that supplement the full-day hourly S / L / IC / EC services. P trains often have different routing and/or stopping patterns than the normal services. Ones which depart before 15:00 have the train number of 7xxx, and ones after 15:00 have the train number of 8xxx. There are also some S trains which are functionally P trains, and have such train numbers.
  • T: T trains are tourism-oreinted services. They have the train numbers of 180xx.
  • (Eurocity Direct to/from the Netherlands cannot be used for domestic travel within Belgium.)
  • (ICE to/from Germany can be taken within Belgium, but the price is very high.)
  • (Ouigo to/from France cannot be used for domestic travel within Belgium.)

S, L, P, T, IC, and EC trains are covered by the same domestic ticketing when travelling within Belgium. (This is different from Germany, where ICE/IC have very different pricing from those of the slower trains.)

Tickets

Belgian Railways sells tickets to all train stations in Belgium with regular train services. The number of tickets do not run out, and there is no seat reservation.

It is best to buy a ticket before boarding a train. Buying a ticket from a train conductor costs an extra €9, payable by electronic payment (on board fare; see that page for some exceptions). If you fail to buy a ticket, you may be fined. As for the physical form of tickets, there are paper tickets and e-tickets. Some season tickets can be loaded to an NFC card.

Paper ticket

The tickets issued by ticket machines, ticket counters, and train conductors are paper tickets. They sell tickets to all domestic train stations with normal train services.

NMBS/SNCB also runs their own trains to some short-distance cross-border destinations, with ticketing that is more similar to domestic ticketing than normal international ticketing. These cross-border tickets can be obtained in all ticket machines and ticket offices. In some of these foreign border stations there are Belgian ticket machines. (In the past, these Belgian ticket machines in foreign country offer a somewhat narrower range of tickets. I don't know whether this is still the case now.)

  • S 32 to/from Roosendaal in the Netherlands. There are Belgian ticket machines in Roosendaal.
  • (Not for Breda and Rotterdam Centraal on EuroCity. These are "proper" international destinations.)
  • All stations on S 43 / RE 18 to Netherlands and Germany. There are Belgian ticket machines in Maastricht and Maastricht Randwyck. There are no Belgian ticket machines in Eijsden, but tickets from Eijsden can be purchased from the conductor without the €9 surcharge (check on board fare).
  • S 41 / RE29 to/from Aachen Hbf in Germany. Only Belgian ticketing applies on S 41 / RE29. There are Belgian ticket machines in Aachen Hbf.
  • Luxembourg via Gouvy border, Sterpenich border, or Athus. (Second class train travel within Luxembourg is fare-free, but border crossing charge still has to be paid, except Athus, which is concidered a domestic tarif point by Luxembourg, i.e. no border crossing charge.)
  • S 63 / K82 to/from Maubeuge in France.
  • (Not for Aulnoye-Aymeries, (Saint-Quentin,) Creil, and Paris Nord on Ouigo. These are "proper" international destinations.)
  • Stations on the IC lines to/from Lille Flandre in France via the Blandain border or Tourcoing border.

A ticket to these cross-border destinations can be purchased from any NMBS / SNCB ticket office, and there is no service charge. This is unlike other "proper" international destinations: (if you want to get a ticket in person instead of online,) tickets to these "proper" international destinations can only be purchased at specialised international ticket counters, and there is a service charge.

E-ticket

Domestic tickets can be purchased at the NMBS / SNCB domestic website or app. Cross-border and international tickets have to be purchased at the international website or app.

NFC card

The Belgian NFC card is called MoBIB, which is shared by NMBS / SNCB and the public transport companies of Flanders, Brussels, and Wallonia. Unlike Netherland's OV-chipkaart, Belgium's MoBIB does not have a credit function, i.e. you cannot "add money" to the card. With other companies, they have time-based tickets that you can load into a MoBIB card. With NMBS / SNCB, most season tickets are loaded into a MoBIB card.

Pricing

Do I have to buy a ticket early? No. There is no discount for early booking for domestic and cross-border travels. There is no seat reservation, and tickets do not run out.

NMBS / SNCB charges by distance in kilometres. NMBS / SNCB has a huge range of different tickets and subscriptions. It is reported that there will be an overhaul of the ticketing system in probably 2025, e.g. a discount subscription that anyone can purchase will be introduced.

There is the concept of "zones". If you hold a domestic ticket, these are single tarif points. If the origin and/or destination is a "zone" (e.g. Zone Brussels is basically the stations in Brussels Capital Region), you have unlimited train travel within the zone before you leave the zone, within the day of train operations. A single ticket from and to Zone Brussels give you unlimited train travel within the zone for the day. This used to be case also for international tickets, but this has been canceled since 15-12-2024.

A ticket to or from one of the Mortsel stations has equal validity at the other two Mortsel stations. (The station of Mortsel-Deurnesteenweg has ceased operations since 15-12-2024.) However, Mortsel is not a "zone", i.e. there is no unlimited travel amongst these.

A domestic return ticket to one of the coastal stations allow the passenger to return from another coastal station. The coastal stations are Knokke, Duinbergen, Heist, Zeebrugge-Dorp, Zeebrugge-Strand, Blankenberge, Oostende, Veurne, Koksijde, and De Panne.

The only station with ticket gates in Belgium is Brussles Airport–Zaventem station. There is a surcharge for going through the station gates at this station, applicable to most passengers. This surcharge is already included in the ordinary single and return tickets, but you may need to purchage this separately in some situations. This surcharge comes in the form of a barcode, or loaded in a MoBIB card.

Fully folded bicycles travel for free. Bicycles are not allowed to board and deboard trains at Brussels-Central, Brussel-Kapellekerk / Bruxelles-Chapelle, and Brussel-Congres / Bruxelles-Congrès.

The terms and conditions can be found here (in Dutch and French).

There is the Euregio ticket, which is valid on public transport in Belgian Limburg and Liège provinces, and large areas in neighbouring Netherlands and Germany.

Travel assistance

See NMBS / SNCB's page of Travel Assistance (domestic) and Travel Assistance (international). NMBS / SNCB launched its first fully accessible trains in 2024.

Belgium: long-distance international

The following is a list of long distance international rail services that run directly to/from Belgium. Many are bookable through B-europe (website, app), or (the international versions of) the websites and apps of the railway companies of neighbouring countries. With all of the services listed below, it is a good idea to book early. With cheaper tickets, you typically have to catch a specific train (check the conditions carefully before purchasing a ticket). (I don't know how complete the following list is.)

  • EuroCity Brussels-South – Rotterdam Centraal. Within Belgium, normal Belgian domestic ticketing applies.
  • Eurocity Direct Brussels-South – Amsterdam Zuid (– Lelystad Centrum). Domestic travel within Belgium is not allowed (but domestic travel within the Netherlands has normal Dutch domestic ticketing). This service is faster than EC, but slower than Eurostar.
  • European Sleeper, night trains (via the Netherlands) to Berlin / Dresden / Prague, and seasonal trains to Venice.
  • ICE International Brussels-South – Frankfurt am Main Hbf. Seat reservation is not compulsory with 2nd class. You can book tickets within Belgium, but the tickets are very expensive.
  • Nightjet of ÖBB (Austrian Railway). Night trains between Belgium and Vienna or Berlin
  • Eurostar primarily to London and Paris Nord, and also to Paris CDG / Disneyland, Holland, and North Rhein-Westphalia. There are also seasonal services to the French Alps and the Mediterranean Coast. Tickets are not bookable for journeys within Belgium. For services to London, UK immigration is conducted before reaching Britain, and it is not possible to book tickets on such services to a destination in France in between.   • TGV INOUI, French high-speed rail to Lille Europe and many other destinations in France. (But not Paris Nord; high-speed rail to Paris Nord is handled by Eurostar.) Network map.

Luxembourg is considered a cross-border destination, and can be reached using ordinary IC, L, and P trains.

There are special tickets for continuing on French local trains (TER) to/from many destinations in Hauts-de-France with the Belgian trains at Lille-Flandres or Maubeuge. See this page for more information.

Luxembourg

"Luxembourg" here refers to the independent country of Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. To the west is the Province of Luxembourg of Belgium, which largely consist of land which used to belong to Luxembourg (a small part of which traditionally spoke Luxembourgish, e.g. Arlon). Luxembourg is Lëtzebuerg in Luxembourgish, and Luxemburg in German and Dutch. The central station is called Luxembourg.

For journey planning in Luxembourg, you can use the domestic CFL website or app, or the mobiliteit.lu website or app.

Companies

Most trains are run by Luxembourgish Railways (Société nationale des chemins de fer luxembourgeois, "CFL". Trains north to Gouvy border and west to Sterpenich border (Arlon) are run in coorperation with Belgian Railways, and Belgian trains are also seen on those lines. Local trains and TGV crossing the Bettembourg border are run by French Railways; local trains by SNCF TER Grand-Est, and TGV by that national branch. Maps above with Luxembourg are repeated here.


Service types

  • RB: all-stop services
  • RE: limited stop services
  • InterCity (IC): usually only stops at larger stations. To the west, non-stop to Arlon (Belgium). To the north, the IC stops at many stops in the north of the country.
  • TER: French local train services, crossing the Bettembourg border. (Can be functionally like RB, RE, or IC.)
  • TGV INOUI: French high speed services, crossing the Bettembourg border. The next stops are (all in France) Thionville, Metz-Ville, and then west on the high speed line to Paris-Est, or east on the high speed line to Strasbourg, and then further to e.g. Lyon, Marseille, Montpellier. Network map.

Run by CFL, one RE per day to/from Koblenz Hbf continues as IC to/from Düsseldorf Hbf. Seats can be booked on this service. Also run by CFL are buses to Saarbrücken Hbf and Lorraine TGV.

Tickets and pricing

Public transport within Luxembourg is fare-free (train, bus, tram, funicular). The exception is first class train travel. Two-hour and day tickets can be purchsed from ticket machines or the domestic CFL app. Bundles of these tickets (better price per ticket) can be purchased at the domestic CFL app.

Bicycles travel for free in Luxembourgish trains. See this page for rules on international trains.

Right across the border, Athus in Belgium, and Audun-le-Tiche and Volmerange-les-Mines in France are domestic tarif points from CFL's point of view. The latter two are not connected to the French passenger rail network.

With the exception of some Wallonian buses, which do not take passengers domestically within Luxembourg, all ordinary cross-border buses are subsummed under the Luxembourgish bus system. Within Luxembourg, these buses are fare-free. For cross-border travel, there are two price-levels: RegioZone 1 and RegioZone 2. See this page for the tickets available. Tickets can be puchased and loaded into mKaart, purchased in the CFL app, at the CFL ticket counters, or from the bus driver.

The Saarbrücken express bus is covered by RegioZone 2 tickets. On the other hand, the express bus to Lorraine TGV has its own ticketting, and requires reservation at least 2.5 hours before departure.

Ticket machines can handle tickets for, e.g. Arlon / Namur / Brussels, (Gouvy / Vielsalm / Trois-Ponts?,) Trier / Koblenz (?), Thionville / Metz / Nacy, and Longwy. The domestic CFL app sells day-return ticket to Trier (Germany).

From 01-01-2025, the Deutschlandticket is valid on all Luxembourg – Germany cross-border buses except the Saarbrücken Express. The Deutschlandticket is already valid on RE / RB trains between Luxembourg and Germany.

There is the Saar-Lor-Lux ticket, which is valid on public transport in Luxembourg, and neighbouring Saarland of Germany and Lorraine of France.

CFL's RE-IC service to Düsseldorf has German pricing. With the RE up to Koblenz Hbf, German local fare applies. If the IC between Koblenz Hbf and Düsseldorf Hbf is involved, German long-distance fare applies, e.g. dynamic pricing (it is better to purchase tickets early). TGV to/from France involves French long-distance ticketing, e.g. dynamic pricing.

There is an international ticket office at Luxembourg station. These are the CFL international booking webpage and International app.

Travel assistance

Please see CFL's page on travel assistance. There is also the Adapto.lu app.

Germany

The most important distinction is between Nahverkehr "local transport" and Fernverkehr "long-distance transport". There are many types of services, but the following are the more-commonly-known local transport service types:

  • S (S-bahn): usually all-stop services around larger urban centres.
  • RB: usually all-stop services elsewhere; may skip some S-bahn stations.
  • RE: limited stops
  • IRE: like IC, but works with local transport tickets

Local-transport train services are primarily funded by state governments. Their fares typically do not fluctuate. The local trains are run by many different companies.

The vast majority of long-distance trains are run by Deutsche Bahn "German Railway" (DB). (There journey planner (website, app) is good for "everything".) The following are the long-distance service types run by DB:

  • IC (InterCity): long distance conventional rail services
  • ICE: long distance high-speed rail services
  • ECE: some international ICE services are called ECE

There is dynamic pricing with long-distance train services, i.e. the prices fluctuate, and it is best to book early. With a cheaper long-distance ticket, you are typically restricted to particular long-distance trains (whereas you can catch any local trains stated in the ticket within the day). Usually, with an ICE / ECE ticket, you can catch IC and local trains running in the same trajectory; with an IC ticket, you can catch local trains running in the same trajectory. (However, read the conditions carefully.)

The local train services are organised by transport associations. Some run bus / train services for a short distance into Benelux. Other than the transport associations, there are also state-wide tariffs. For instance, each state has its own day ticket that is valid in "all" local-traffic public transport in at least their state. The German states that border the Netherlands are Niedersachsen "Lower Saxony" and Nordrhein-Westfalen "North Rhein–Westphalia" (NRW). The German states that border Belgium are NRW and Rheinland-Pfalz "Rhineland-Palatinate". The German states that border Luxembourg are Rheinland-Pfalz and Saarland. The Niedersachsenticket and the SchönertagTicket NRW (single or 5 people have some validity in the Netherlands. (There is also a plus Groningen version of the Niedersachsenticket for trains (now replacement buses) towards Groningen.) The Rheinland-Pfalz-Ticket is valid on trains and many buses into Luxembourg. (Saarland-ticket has the same validity as Rheinland-Pfalz-Ticket). However, the German state tickets have very little validity in Belgium, only on a very small number of cross-border buses.

Often more useful is the DeutschlandTicket, which is valid throughout Germany on local public transport in a calendar month. The price is €58 per month in 2025. The DeutschlandTicket is sold by a great number of different German public transport authorities, and they may have slightly different sales conditions. The DeutschlandTicket is a subscription; you have to stop the subscription before a certain date (typically around the 10th of a month), if not you will be charged for the next month as well. The mo.pla app allows you to pause or cancel a subscription right till before the last day of a calendar month.

The DeutschlandTicket has some validity outside Germany, and on some sections of long-distance train services. This is summarised in the following diagram. (See the fine prints in German here (Tarifbestimmungen für das Deutschland-Ticket inkl. SPNV-Geltungsbereiche).)


The validity of these various tickets cannot be used to infer the validity of another. For instance, that a section in the Netherlands is valid with a German state ticket does not mean that it is necessarily also valid with the Deutschland ticket. Read the terms and conditions carefully. In the DB app, it is possible to restrict the search to "Local/regional transport only" or "Long-distance travel only". Within a journey search, it also states explicitely if, e.g., the Deutschland ticket is valid on a specific section of long distance train service.

Totally independent of all of the aforementioned is FlixTrain, which has its own ticketing. Booking early is recommended.

France

The national railway company of France is Société nationale des chemins de fer français (SNCF). The train services run by the regions are called TER (or Transilien in Île-de-France / Paris region). Services run at the national level include TGV INOUI (high speed), intercité (long distance conventional rail), intercité de nuit (domestic night sleeper services). This is a map of the intercité (de nuit) network. For these services, it is best to book early.

The two French regions that border Belgium and Luxembourg – Hauts-de-France (northern France) and Grand-Est (northeastern France) – have taken over all the intercité-like services, so they are now all TERs. TGV also runs in these regions (and also to Brussels and Luxembourg). Bookings can be done at the national SNCF website / app, or the regional websites of TER Hauts-de-France and TER Grand-Est. Both TER Hauts-de-France and TER Grand Est have cheap tickets, so it is best to book earlier. TER Hauts-de-France has TERGV, which is normal TER price plus a small surcharge for certain TGV services within the region.

OUIGO is the low-cost brand of SNCF. There are high-speed-rail and conventional-rail services. Like low-cost airlines, you can only book online, and it is better to book earlier. OUIGO's conventional-rail service between Brussels-South and Paris Nord begins on 19-12-2024.
