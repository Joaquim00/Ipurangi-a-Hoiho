\section{Middle Chinese}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Middle Chinese}
The vast majority of linguistic material in Cantonese can be traced to Middle Chinese\\
\bigskip
\enquote{Early Middle Chinese} {\footnotesize in Chinese historical phonology}:\\
《切韻》qièyùn / cit3 wan5  (rime book; 601 隋)\\
\smallskip
\quad {\footnotesize Only parts of it have survived; but there are many augmented versions}\\
\quad {\footnotesize e.g.《廣韻》guǎngyùn / gwong2 wan5 (1008; official rime book of 宋)}\\
\bigskip
Author of《切韻》: 陸法言 lù fǎyán / luk6 faat3 jin4 \textsuperscript{鮮卑} \\  
\bigskip
{\footnotesize 隋 Suí (581-618) unified China in 589 CE after \(\approx \) 300 years of disunity\\
\medskip
Eight scholars (friends of 陸法言's father) were trying to create a new unified standard, based on a compromise of the \enquote{best} pronunciation of characters from the old northern and southern standards\\}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{MC Tones}

Tone splitting:\\ 
e.g. Proto-Yue\\
/taj\textsuperscript{C}/ [taj˧]\\
/daj\textsuperscript{C}/ [daj˧]\\
\medskip
tone-splitting may occur:\\
/taj\textsuperscript{C}/ [taj˧]\\
/daj\textsuperscript{C}/ [daj˨] {\scriptsize (voiced onset lowering pitch)}\\
\medskip
\textit{\scriptsize In most, but not all, Sinitic languages, voiced initials have become voiceless}\\
tone-splitting becomes phonemicised:\\
/taj\textsuperscript{C1}/ /taj˧/ Canto. 帶 daai3\\
/taj\textsuperscript{C2}/ /taj˨/ Canto. 大 daai6\\
\medskip
cf. Cantonese tone 3 /˧/ 6 /˨/ both descended from MC tone C\\
cf. Standard Mandarin where MC tone C has not split:\\
\quad 帶 dài, 大 dài (as in 大夫 dài fu)

\begin{frame}[fragile]{MC initials \& Late Middle Chinese}
Early Middle Chinese:《切韻》 cit3 wan5 / qièyùn (601 隋)\\ (artificial: compromise between N and S Standard Chinese)\\
\smallskip
organised by tones and finals\\ 
no organised information on the initials\\

\smallskip

information on tones and finals: basically the same as《切韻》\\

\medskip
{\footnotesize (There are rime tables like《四聲等子》(1039 北宋) and《切韻指掌圖》(1230 南宋) which reflected pronunciation at the time better)}\\

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Early MC initials}

Some other notes:\\
\begin{itemize}
    \item not all were phonemically contrastive, e.g. 泥 [n] 娘 [ɳ] were in complementary distribution
    \item in Canto., 清 voiceless = high tone, 濁 voiced = low tone\\
    (全濁 voiced obstruent became voiceless)
    \item Traditional \textbf{並} > Simplified \textbf{并}; but\\
    Mainland China writes MC initial [b] as \textbf{並}\\
    (Simp. \textbf{并} < Trad. \textbf{并} MC [p] or \textbf{併} [p/b] or \textbf{並} [b])
    \item 俟 MC [ʐ] only has two characters (俟漦) in 切韻, both merged into 崇 [ɖʐ] in 廣韻
    \item {\scriptsize (excluding 俟)} in Cantonese,\\ MC retroflex 莊初崇生 (zong1 co1 sung4 sang1) often have back vowels\\ MC palatal 章昌常書船 (zoeng1 coeng1 soeng4 syu1 syun4) often have front vowels (\underline{rules not 100\%})
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Late MC initials}
Some other points:
\begin{itemize}
    \item voiced obstruents became breathy, and on their way to become voiceless
    \item labiodentals 非敷奉微 developed out of bilabials 幫滂並明\\ (輕唇 hing1 seon4 vs. 重唇 cung5 seon4 respectively)
    \item 非敷 were earlier [pf] [pfʰ]; soon they merged to [f]
    \item Cantonese usually have /f/ for 非敷奉;\\ some /p//pʰ/ are retained, e.g.\\ 番 faan1 but 番禺 pun1 jyu4 (Pānyú)\\ 伏 fuk6 but 伏匿匿 buk6 nei1 nei1
    \item 微 TW Mand. wéi, BJ Mand. wēi. However, when referring to the MC initial 微, N Chinese people may change their pronunciation to 微 wéi 
\end{itemize}
