\begin{frame}[fragile]{Kra-Dai influence: phonology}

\underline{Having ɬ (or θ)}\\
    De Sousa (2015: 166-168):\\ ɬ in Sinitic languages = reflexes of MC *s\\ ɬ in Tai languages = reflexes of PT *s\\ Chin. \& Thai linguists attributes \textsuperscript{Guangxi} ɬ to each other's family.\\
    \medskip
    {\footnotesize Most Tai linguists reconstructed *s instead of *ɬ;\\ *ʰl is needed, and *ɬ cannot coexist due to phonetic similarity}\\
    \medskip
    Liao(2023: 128-136)'s comment on \enquote{De Sousa's puzzle}\\
    PT should be reconstructed with *ʰl vs. *ɬ (and not *s):\\
    1. *ʰl and *ɬ coexist in Southern Guangxi, e.g.\\ in many 邕南壯 varieties ʰlaj˩˧\textsuperscript{B1} \enquote*{egg} (Thai \begin{thai}ไข่\end{thai} kʰaj˨)\\ vs. ɬaj˥ \textsuperscript{A1us} \enquote*{snail} (>螺\underline{螄}粉)\\
    2. *ɬ is 0 or 1 step away from its reflexes: ɬ θ ʰɹ tʰ s {\scriptsize(\& s > sʰ)}\\
    \medskip
    i.e. Tai ɬ > Sinitic ɬ \textsuperscript{in Guangxi / Guangdong} 

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Kra-Dai influence: phonology}

\underline{Vowel length distinction}\\
The vast majority of Pan-Yue dialects have long vs. short vowels\\
\medskip
The only Sinitic group to have vowel-length contrast.\\
\medskip
\footnotesize{徽: e.g. 屯溪\\ 
<ie> 雞入 <iːe> 鱉便\\ 
<ue> 揮毀 <uːə> 花板發\\  
<ye> 桂惠 <yːe> 專兄\\   
\smallskip
However, this is not vowel length distinction:\\
/je/ vs. /iə̯/\\
/we/ vs. /uə̯/\\
/ɥe/ vs. /yə̯/\\
\textit{(Chinese linguistics traditionally writes medial glides with vowel symbols, and hence e.g. [ye̯] needs to written as something other than <ye>)}
\medskip
\begin{tabular}{c c c}
glide (G) & nucleus (V) & coda (C)\\ \hline
/j w ɥ/ & /a o e ɤ ɛ/ & \\
 & /i u y a ɛ/ & /ə̯ w n/\\
\end{tabular}\\
\textit{(not all GV \& VC combinations available; GVC: /jaw/ /jan/ /ɥan/)}
} 

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Kra-Dai influence: phonology}

\underline{Vowel length distinction}\\
The vast majority of Pan-Yue dialects have long vs. short vowels: *iC > *iɐC > ɐC\\
/ɐ/ phonetically short; interpret as short counterpart of /a/, which is phonetically long.\\
\smallskip
\textit{(Other sound changes created other long/short vowel pairs)}\\
\smallskip
Vowel length contrast reconstructed in all branches of Kra-Dai (except Proto-Kra)\\

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Kra-Dai influence: phonology}

{\small
\underline{Spliting of Tone D based on vowel length}\\
A majority of Pan-Yue dialects split at least one of the D tones based on vowel length, e.g. Cantonese 筆 /pɐt˥/ (MC pit\textsuperscript{D}) 八 /pat˧/ (MC pɛt\textsuperscript{D})\\
\medskip
The vast majority of Kra-Dai languages have a split in tone D based on vowel length (except most Kra and Hlai languages).\\
The DL-DS area of Kra-Dai and Pan-Yue are joined.\\
\medskip
This phenomenon much more wide-spread in Kra-Dai than Sinitic; spread from Kra-Dai to (Pealr River) Sinitic(?)\\
\medskip
But note: earliest record of Cantonese having DL-DS split: \\陳澧 1851《東塾初學編．音學》\\
earliest record of Thai having DL-DS split: \\Pallegoix 1850 \textit{Grammatica linguæ Thai} (Pittayaporn 2018)\\
\smallskip
Two separate events? One related event?\\
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Kra-Dai influence: grammar}

\textit{good \underline{book}, \underline{on} [\underline{top} of the table], \underline{the man} who eats cake}\\
\bigskip
SVO languages in core of MSEA: NP strongly left-headed, e.g. Viet.\\
\textit{\underline{sách}\textsuperscript{冊} hay, \underline{trên} [\underline{mặt} bàn\textsuperscript{盤}], \underline{người đàn ông}\textsuperscript{翁} ăn bánh\textsuperscript{餅}}\\
\bigskip
Sinitic languages: mainly SVO, but NP strongly right headed:, e.g.\\
Canto.\\
\textit{hou1 \underline{syu1}} 好\underline{書}\\

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Kra-Dai influence: grammar}

Chappell (2015)'s five Sinitic linguistic areas:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Northern\\
    \item Central Transitional (e.g. Jiānghuái / Lw Yangtze Mand.)\\

    {\scriptsize Pínghuà is somewhat between Far S and SW}
\end{itemize}
Based on comparative, passive, object marking constructions.

{\footnotesize
\textbf{Object marking construction} {\scriptsize (disposal construction)}:\\
\medskip
Standard Mandarin\\
wǒ bǎ shū nòng diū le 我把書弄丟了\\
\textsc{1sg} \textsc{om} book make be.lost \textsc{prf}\\
\enquote*{I have lost the book.}\\
\smallskip
Standard Cantonese\\
ngo5 m4gin3 zo2 bun2 syu1 我唔見咗本書\\
\textsc{1sg} lost \textsc{pfv} \textsc{clf} book\\
\enquote*{I lost a/the book.}\\
\smallskip
(Standard Cantonese does have an \textsc{om} constrution marked by 將 zoeng1)\\
\smallskip
Nánníng Cantonese: does not have a grammaticalised \textsc{om} construction (Lin \& Qin 2008: 344-348)\\
\medskip
Chappell (2015: 22)'s Far Southern area:\\
Sinitic \textsc{om} not used much, or at most using a non-grammaticalised or lightly grammaticalised \textsc{take} serial verb construction; similar to e.g. Thai, Khmer, Vietnamese, Hmong (Bisang 1992).\\
